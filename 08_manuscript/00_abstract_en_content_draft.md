# ABSTRACT

This study investigates the scientific-thinking requirements of the theoretical problems in the second-round papers from the 35th to the 42nd editions of the National Physics Competition for High School Students in China and derives implications for instructional design. Analytically distinct subquestions yielded 219 units and 876 final coding records. Content analysis identified the presence of model construction, scientific reasoning, scientific argumentation, and questioning and innovation, together with their SOLO-inspired task-structure levels. Two anonymized coding submissions were compared and then subjected to disagreement adjudication and record-level evidence review. Dimensional presence, level composition, and co-occurrence were described, and six representative cases were examined using item statements, diagrams, and reference solutions. The four dimensions occurred in 203, 219, 106, and 84 units, respectively. L1 was the most frequent level for model construction, scientific reasoning, and scientific argumentation, whereas L2 was most frequent for questioning and innovation. The binary scientific-reasoning indicator was saturated in this sample; its discriminating information therefore came from the 121, 81, and 17 units at L1, L2, and L3 and from their different relation structures, rather than from the 100% presence rate. Scientific argumentation and questioning and innovation co-occurred in 40 units. The cases revealed condition-relation chains, state classification and comparison, approximation-based modeling, same-object comparison, multi-object constraints, and threshold judgment. These structures supported an instructional-design chain linking task structure, learning task, scaffold, observable product, and feedback focus. The findings connect subquestion-level statistics with concrete physical mechanisms and describe task requirements represented by the items and reference solutions rather than students' actual capabilities or instructional effectiveness.

Keywords: physics competition; scientific thinking; content analysis; task cognitive requirements; instructional design
