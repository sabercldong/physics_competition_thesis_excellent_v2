# ABSTRACT

This study investigates the scientific-thinking requirements of the theoretical problems in the second-round papers from the 35th to the 42nd editions of the National Physics Competition for High School Students in China and derives implications for instructional design. Subquestions with independent response goals yielded 221 analytical units and 884 records in the V3 unique coding dataset. For each unit, content analysis identified the presence of model construction, scientific reasoning, scientific argumentation, and questioning and innovation, together with their SOLO-inspired task-structure levels. Each record retained evidence from the item statement and reference solution, source locations, and the rationale for excluding adjacent levels. Following an item-by-item substantive review by the author, all descriptive tables and figures were generated from the same version-controlled dataset. Model construction, scientific reasoning, scientific argumentation, and questioning and innovation occurred in 202, 221, 36, and 2 units, accounting for 91.40%, 100.00%, 16.29%, and 0.90% of the sample. Among units in which each dimension was present, the L1/L2/L3 counts were 9/77/116 for model construction, 13/69/139 for scientific reasoning, and 14/16/6 for scientific argumentation; both instances of questioning and innovation were coded L2, and no L4 instance occurred in any dimension. The binary scientific-reasoning indicator was saturated in this sample, so its discriminating information lies in task-structure levels and specific physical relations rather than in the 100% presence rate. Model construction and scientific reasoning co-occurred in 202 units, whereas all four dimensions co-occurred in only 2 units. Six representative cases revealed condition-relation chains, state classification and comparison, approximation-based modeling, same-object comparison, multi-object constraints, and threshold judgment. These structures supported an instructional-design chain linking task structure, learning task, scaffold, observable product, and feedback focus. The findings connect subquestion-level descriptive statistics with concrete physical mechanisms and describe requirements represented by the items and reference solutions rather than students' actual capabilities or instructional effectiveness.

Keywords: physics competition; scientific thinking; content analysis; task cognitive requirements; instructional design
