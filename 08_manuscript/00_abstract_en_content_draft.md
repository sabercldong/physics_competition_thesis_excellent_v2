# ABSTRACT

This study examines scientific-thinking requirements in the theoretical problems from the 35th to the 42nd editions of the National Physics Competition for High School Students in China and derives implications for instructional design. Subquestions with independent response goals yielded 221 analytical units and 884 V3 coding records. Each record retained evidence from the item statement and reference solution, source locations, and a rationale for excluding adjacent levels, and all records were reviewed item by item by the author. Model construction, scientific reasoning, scientific argumentation, and questioning and innovation occurred in 202, 221, 36, and 2 units, respectively, accounting for 91.40%, 100.00%, 16.29%, and 0.90% of the sample. Within units where each dimension was present, the L1/L2/L3 counts were 9/77/116 for model construction, 13/69/139 for scientific reasoning, and 14/16/6 for scientific argumentation; both questioning-and-innovation instances were L2, and no L4 instance occurred. Reweighting by original subquestion and major problem changed the four presence rates by at most 1.40 percentage points without altering their rank order. Six main cases and a paired comparison of adjacent Mossbauer-effect subquestions illustrated condition-relation chains, state classification, approximation-based modeling, same-object comparison, multi-object constraints, threshold judgment, and measurement-scheme design. These findings informed a task-structure-to-feedback design chain, a 45-minute lesson, and a four-dimension artifact rubric covering physical correctness, relational completeness, sufficiency of justification, and awareness of model boundaries. The study connects descriptive statistics with concrete physical mechanisms and offers a traceable basis for competition-problem research and instructional design.

Keywords: physics competition; scientific thinking; content analysis; task cognitive requirements; instructional design
