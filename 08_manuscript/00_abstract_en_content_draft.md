# ABSTRACT

This study investigates the scientific-thinking requirements of the theoretical problems in the second-round papers from the 35th to the 42nd editions of the National Physics Competition for High School Students in China and derives implications for instructional design. Analytically distinct subquestions were used as units of analysis, yielding 219 units and 876 coding records. Content analysis was used to identify the presence of four dimensions--model construction, scientific reasoning, scientific argumentation, and questioning and innovation--and their SOLO-inspired task-structure levels. After independent coding by two raters, adjudication of disagreements, and record-level evidence review, descriptive statistics were produced for dimensional presence, level composition, and co-occurrence. Representative cases were then examined using item statements, diagrams, and reference solutions. Model construction, scientific reasoning, scientific argumentation, and questioning and innovation occurred in 203, 219, 106, and 84 units, respectively. Scientific reasoning formed a common baseline requirement across this corpus, while its levels ranged from direct relation use to multi-condition integration. Model construction was not treated as an automatic property of every physics problem: 16 units were completed within a fully specified model. L1 was the most frequent level for model construction, scientific reasoning, and scientific argumentation, whereas L2 was the most frequent level for questioning and innovation. Model construction and scientific reasoning co-occurred in 203 units, and scientific argumentation and questioning and innovation co-occurred in 40 units. Six representative cases illustrated condition-relation chains, state classification and comparison, approximation-based modeling, same-object comparison, multi-object constraints, and threshold judgment. These structures supported an instructional-design chain linking task structure, learning task, scaffold, observable product, and feedback focus. The study connects subquestion-level statistics with concrete physical mechanisms instead of using content topics, formula counts, or calculation length as proxies for scientific thinking. Its findings describe task requirements represented by the items and reference solutions and do not directly indicate students' actual capabilities or instructional effectiveness.

Keywords: physics competition; scientific thinking; content analysis; task cognitive requirements; instructional design
