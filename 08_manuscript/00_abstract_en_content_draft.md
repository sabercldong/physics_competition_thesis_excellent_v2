# ABSTRACT

This study investigates the scientific-thinking requirements of the theoretical problems in the second-round papers from the 35th to the 42nd editions of the National Physics Competition for High School Students in China and derives implications for instructional design. Analytically distinct subquestions yielded 219 units and 876 coding records. Content analysis identified the presence of model construction, scientific reasoning, scientific argumentation, and questioning and innovation, together with their SOLO-inspired task-structure levels. Following independent coding by two raters, disagreement adjudication, and record-level evidence review, dimensional presence, level composition, and co-occurrence were described, and six representative cases were examined using item statements, diagrams, and reference solutions. The four dimensions occurred in 203, 219, 106, and 84 units, respectively. L1 was the most frequent level for model construction, scientific reasoning, and scientific argumentation, whereas L2 was most frequent for questioning and innovation. Scientific reasoning formed a common baseline requirement but ranged from direct relation use to multi-condition integration; 16 units were completed within a fully specified model, showing that model construction was not assigned automatically. Model construction and scientific reasoning co-occurred in 203 units, while scientific argumentation and questioning and innovation co-occurred in 40 units. The cases revealed condition-relation chains, state classification and comparison, approximation-based modeling, same-object comparison, multi-object constraints, and threshold judgment. These structures supported an instructional-design chain linking task structure, learning task, scaffold, observable product, and feedback focus. The findings connect subquestion-level statistics with concrete physical mechanisms and describe task requirements represented by the items and reference solutions rather than students' actual capabilities or instructional effectiveness.

Keywords: physics competition; scientific thinking; content analysis; task cognitive requirements; instructional design
